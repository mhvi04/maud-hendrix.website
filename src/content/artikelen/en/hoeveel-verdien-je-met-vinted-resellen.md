Search TikTok for Vinted reselling and within five minutes you'll find someone claiming they earn thousands of euros a month with secondhand clothing. What you rarely find is someone putting their full numbers on the table. Including the slow first years, the costs nobody counts, and the difference between what the app shows and what lands in your bank account.

These are my numbers. Four years, more than 2,500 transactions, no rounding up.

## The yearly figures, unfiltered

| Year | Items sold | Yearly revenue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2022 | 53 | €917 |
| 2023 | — | €4,420 |
| 2024 | — | €10,974 |
| 2025 | 1,039 (774 orders) | €35,102 |

In 2022 I sold 53 pieces for a yearly revenue of €917. That's not a typo. My first real year brought in less than a two-weekend student job. In 2023 that grew to €4,420, the year I focused on Ralph Lauren and my first sourcing logic emerged. In 2024 it became €10,974. In 2025 I processed 774 orders totalling 1,039 items, for a gross revenue of €35,102.

That curve reads like a success story, but pay attention to what it actually says. It took three full years before this produced something that started to resemble an income. Anyone starting today and expecting serious amounts within six months is banking on someone else's highlight reel.

## Revenue isn't what you get

That €35,102 is the figure the Vinted export shows. What actually got paid out landed around €29,736. The difference, about 15 percent of gross revenue, disappears into cancelled orders, refunds, bundle adjustments and shipping items. Beginners look at their sales overview in the app and think that's their revenue. It's a starting point, not a final figure.

From what's left, stock costs come off next. My average gross margin in 2025 was 56 percent. So out of every euro that came in, 56 cents remained after subtracting the direct purchase cost of the item. That sounds generous until you realise everything else still has to be paid from it. Packaging material, roughly €0.50 per item. Software and admin. The bad buys that never sell or go out the door at a loss. And if you do this as a self-employed person in Belgium, [taxes and social contributions](/en/articles/belasting-betalen-op-vinted-in-belgie) that together add up to roughly 45 percent of your result.

## The calculation beginners make, and why it's wrong

Take a T-shirt I bought for €6 and sold for €25. Gross profit €19, and it took me roughly 25 to 35 minutes total for sourcing, checking, photography, description, communication and shipping. That looks like it comes out to 38 euros an hour.

That number is correct, but it's not an hourly wage. It only measures the active processing time of that one successful item. It doesn't count the search hours where you found nothing. Not the item that sat for four months. Not the return that cost you three times the margin you'd have made on it. Not the 19 percent of my orders that never completed due to cancellations or payment issues, while the packaging time had already been spent.

If you want to know what you actually earn, at the end of a period you take your total net profit and divide it by all the hours worked. Including the hours you spent doubting, answering messages and hauling packages to the drop-off point. In my case, that came out in 2025, after Belgian taxes and social contributions, to 21 to 27 euros net per hour. Still a good number. But a different number than 38, and the gap between those two is exactly what most reselling content leaves out.

## How much time this actually takes

In 2025 I worked between 23 and 31 hours a week on reselling, alongside my studies. That's not a side hustle you handle on a Sunday evening. There were peak days where I shipped fifty packages at once. Every item that goes online has first been sourced, checked for authenticity, washed, ironed, photographed, measured and described.

If you don't count the time, you always come out looking profitable on paper. The question isn't whether you make a profit per item. The question is what you keep per hour worked, across all items, including the failures.

## A realistic set of expectations

Don't take a promise from my numbers, take a pattern. Year one is tuition money: you make sourcing mistakes, you don't yet know market prices, and your revenue stays small. Anyone who works systematically can see steady growth in years two and three, provided the margin stays intact. My growth from 2024 to 2025 didn't come from discounting or cutting margin, it came from doing more of what demonstrably worked. Gross margin stayed stable around 50 to 56 percent over that period, even as volume tripled.

And even then, the conclusion stays sober. An average order value of €45.35, a margin of 56 percent, and dozens of hours a week got me a net hourly wage in my best year comparable to a solid job. Not a goldmine. But a real business, with real numbers, that works as long as you treat it as one — with the [hidden costs](/en/articles/verborgen-kosten-van-vinted) that weigh slightly differently at every price segment, and the tax reality that only kicks in once you're actually buying stock to resell, not just [clearing out your own closet](/en/articles/de-vinted-vrijstelling).

> Do you really make money reselling on Vinted? Yes. Do you make it fast, passively or effortlessly? No. Anyone who claims otherwise shows you the revenue and keeps the rest of the math to themselves.
