You listed something you genuinely liked. The photos were fine, the price seemed reasonable, and still, nothing happens. No hearts, no messages, the days tick by. Almost every beginner has the same reflex: drop the price. That's often not the right first move, and sometimes it's the exact move that shrinks your odds of selling.

I treat every item I list according to a fixed timeline. Not because I love rules, but because my own sales data shows that stepping in too early and waiting too long both cost money. Here's that timeline, step by step.

| Period | Action |
| --- | --- |
| Day 1 to 3 | Do nothing, let the algorithm's test phase run its course |
| Day 4 to 7 | Monitor, don't intervene |
| Day 8 to 14 | First targeted 5% price cut, preferably Friday evening |
| Day 15 to 21 | The question shifts from price to sellability, max. 10% total reduction |
| Day 22 and beyond | Relist, bundle, or leave it up until the season turns |

## Day 1 to 3: do nothing

The first days after upload, an item gets temporary extra visibility. This is the phase where the system tests your item with a small group of buyers to see if it lands. If you adjust the price during this period, you disrupt that test moment for nothing. Leave the full asking price and watch.

## Day 4 to 7: monitor, don't intervene

The visibility boost starts to flatten, but the item still has traction from those first days. If hearts or messages come in during this period, that means interest that hasn't converted into a sale yet. That's not a problem that calls for a price cut. Patience works better here than intervening.

## Day 8 to 14: the first targeted adjustment

This is the point where a price cut actually makes sense, and the reason why is less obvious than you'd think. The moment you drop the price, Vinted sends a notification to everyone who saved the item as a favourite. That notification, "price drop on an item you liked", generates a fresh wave of visibility. In my data, a 5 percent cut on day 8 raises the odds of selling by 23 percent.

Importantly, that 5 percent isn't meant to tip the buyer over financially. On a €47.50 cable knit, 5 percent is less than €2.50, that convinces nobody on price alone. It works because it sends a new signal to the algorithm and a new notification to interested buyers. Timing that adjustment helps too: Friday evening, so you're visible right before the weekend traffic.

## Day 15 to 21: the question shifts

If the item still hasn't moved after two weeks, the question is no longer whether the price is too high. The question is whether the item is even sellable in its current form. Maybe the photo isn't strong enough, maybe the title isn't discoverable, maybe the season is working against you. A total price cut of maximum 10 percent from the original price is the ceiling here. Beyond that, you start giving away margin for a problem that might have nothing to do with price.

## Zero views is a different problem than views but no sale

These two situations look alike but call for opposite approaches. If you have zero views after a week, that's not a visibility problem that needs patience, it's a matching problem. Your item simply isn't reaching the buyers searching for it, usually because of a title or photo that gives the algorithm too little to work with. Waiting doesn't fix that. Relisting, with a sharper title and better photos, does.

If instead you're getting plenty of views but not a single heart or message, the problem sits elsewhere. Buyers see the item, click through, and bounce, the same reflex that shows up in [the psychology of your buyer](/en/articles/psychologie-van-je-koper/): someone who's hesitant doesn't respond to more visibility, they respond to more certainty. That usually points to a price that doesn't match comparable listings, or photos that don't build enough trust once someone looks closely.

## Day 22 and beyond: three options, no panic

If an item still hasn't moved after three weeks, you have three realistic choices. Relist, removing the item and putting it back up with new photos, an adjusted title and a fresh start in the algorithm. Bundle it with another item to make the total order value more attractive. Or accept that this particular piece is a slow seller and leave it up until the season turns.

Keep in mind that turnaround time varies a lot by category. Calling an item a failure after ten days works differently depending on the category.

| Category | Typical turnaround |
| --- | --- |
| T-shirt | 7 to 14 days |
| Cable knit sweater | 14 to 37 days |
| Iconic pieces (flag knits, bear knits) | Several months |

If you still haven't used a [boost](/en/articles/boosten-op-vinted/) by day 22, it's best to do it after this re-evaluation, not instead of it: a boost accelerates existing interest, it doesn't create it.

## What you should never do

The biggest mistake I see beginners make is adjusting the price daily or every few days with no pattern. Vinted tracks price changes, and frequent, illogical adjustments undermine your listing's stability. What's more, a buyer who sees your item pass by repeatedly with a lower price each time learns to wait. The logic is predictable: if it'll be cheaper again tomorrow, why buy today?

A price cut is a tool you deploy deliberately at fixed moments, not a panic reaction to every quiet day. Follow the timeline, distinguish between a visibility problem and a conversion problem, and leave the first three days alone. Most items that "aren't selling" don't have a price problem at all. They just haven't had a fair chance yet.
