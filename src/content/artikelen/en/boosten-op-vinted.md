I've spent a total of €482 on boosts on Vinted. That sounds like a fair amount for what's essentially a button that temporarily pushes your listing to the top. I'm not sharing that number to claim boosts automatically pay off. A boost doesn't turn a weak listing into a strong one. But on the right listing, it can add real momentum.

Still, this isn't an ode to boosting. Because the return depends entirely on how you use it. I got it wrong myself, plenty of times, not just once. It wasn't until I started comparing my own boost data against the sales results that followed that the pattern became visible.

That pattern shows boosting is a precision tool. Use it too broadly, too long, or at the wrong moment, and you're throwing money away. Use it deliberately, and it's the most efficient marketing budget a small Vinted reseller has access to.

## How a boost actually works

Vinted lets sellers pay to make a listing temporarily more visible. A boosted item gets pushed back to the top of the catalogue daily for three or seven days, and shown more often in the feeds of potential buyers.

That sounds simple, but there's a mechanism behind it that goes beyond visibility alone. Vinted's algorithm, which runs on a system called Vespa Vector Search, links interaction to visibility. The more people click on an item, save it as a favourite, or send a message, the higher it climbs in organic results. A boost gives that first push. Not the sale itself, but the momentum that leads to one.

That distinction matters. A boost doesn't sell an item. A boost gets the item seen by the right people at the right moment, and then the listing itself does the work.

## The big difference nobody tells you: 3 days versus 7 days

Of my 206 boosts, 182 were the 3-day type. 24 were 7-day boosts. The results are further apart than you'd expect.

The 3-day boost ended in a sale 93 percent of the time. The 7-day boost, only 30 percent.

That seems counterintuitive. Longer should mean more, right? Reality is the exact opposite. After 48 hours, an item loses 60 to 70 percent of its organic visibility on average. A boost gives that item a fresh start, a kind of reset on that window. But that window doesn't last seven days. It lasts 48 hours. After that, decay sets in again, even on a boosted item.

A 7-day boost is too long for what the platform actually does. You're paying for five extra days of visibility that keep delivering less and less. A 3-day boost is a sprint at the exact moment the platform is receptive to it. Short, targeted, effective.

Boost costs average between €2 and €3. That's less than one percent of the average transaction value in my closet. Revenue returned per euro invested: €93. That's the ROI of a 3-day boost when it leads to a sale, which makes it by far the most efficient euro you can put into your Vinted operation, provided you boost the right items at the right moment.

## When the boost effect actually kicks in

Here's something most resellers don't know, myself included when I started. The boost effect isn't linear.

After day 1 of a boost, about 10 percent of the eventual effect has materialised. Days 2 and 3 together bring another 20 to 22 percent. But 68 to 70 percent of the total boost effect shows up on days 4 to 7 after the boost, in other words, after the visibility period itself has ended.

What you're actually seeing: the boost generates views and favourites. Those favourites stay put. And the same people come back in the days after to buy, sometimes as late as five days later when they search for that item again. The boost has a tail effect that outlasts the boost itself.

That means you shouldn't judge a boost's success after two days. The full picture only becomes visible after a week.

## Which items I boost, and which I don't

Not every item deserves a boost. That might be the single most valuable lesson from my 206 boosts.

Cable knit sweaters are my primary boost category. They have proven demand, a clear search field, and a price that comfortably justifies the boost cost. If a cable knit hasn't sold after five to seven days but does have favourites, a boost is the logical next step. The interest is already there. The boost sets that interest in motion.

I almost never boost T-shirts. The margin per piece is lower, and organic turnover is already fast enough. A T-shirt that hasn't sold within two weeks usually has a different problem, the price, the photo, or the description, and a boost won't fix that.

For Polo Bear sweaters and flag knits, the decision is different. Those items have a longer life cycle, sometimes 60 to 238 days. A boost on day seven is too early for them. The buyer for that kind of piece is actively and deliberately searching; they don't need the boost to find it. I only boost this type of item once it's been listed for several weeks and views have stagnated.

The rule of thumb I use: only boost when there's already evidence people are interested. Favourites are that evidence, and [what you do with those favourites](/en/articles/favoriet-hartje-op-vinted) often determines whether a boost is even needed. An item with zero favourites doesn't get boosted, because a boost widens visibility but can't fix a lack of interest. That's a listing problem, not a visibility problem.

## What day of the week I boost on

Timing matters here just as much as choosing the right item.

I mostly boost on Thursday or Friday. Thursday is my weakest sales day, good for only 8 percent of weekly volume. If an item gets boosted on Thursday afternoon, it has two to three days to build visibility before the big weekend buying wave. The boost generates views on Friday and Saturday, and those views regularly converted on Sunday, the absolute sales peak.

I never boost on Sunday. Sunday is already the day with the highest volume on its own. Items sell better organically that day than at any other point in the week. A boost on Sunday is wasted budget. You're paying for extra visibility on a day when visibility is already maxed out anyway.

## The mistake I've repeatedly seen other sellers make

There's a pattern I had myself for a while too: boosting items the moment they go live.

The logic sounds reasonable. You want your new item to be visible right away. But it's exactly the wrong order. A brand-new listing already benefits from the "fresh item effect". The algorithm automatically gives new items a temporary visibility boost. If you pay to boost on top of that, you're paying for something that's already happening for free.

The right order is the reverse. Let the item start organically. Give it the first five to seven days to collect views and favourites. If it hasn't sold by then but there has been interest, step in with a boost. At that point, a boost does what it's supposed to do: turn existing interest into action.

## What a boost doesn't fix

A boost is not a band-aid for a bad listing.

If the photos are unclear, if the price is too high relative to comparable listings, if the description is missing, or the title doesn't contain the keywords buyers actually use, more visibility won't help. More people will see the item and ignore it anyway. In that case, [the day-by-day path for an item that isn't selling](/en/articles/vinted-item-verkoopt-niet-wat-nu) helps more than a boost.

I experienced this concretely once with a shirt that had been listed for three weeks without a single favourite. I boosted it. More people saw it. Not a single click. Not a single message. The item was eventually relisted with new photos and an adjusted price, and sold organically within four days. The €2.50 boost I'd spent earlier was wasted.

Some items might have sold organically anyway, just later. Direct causality is hard to isolate. What I do know for certain: the items I boosted sold faster than the items I left organic, and speed is a financial advantage in its own right in the resell economy. Capital that isn't tied up in unsold stock can be redeployed.

> Boosting isn't magic. It's an expense that proves its return when you deploy it at the right moment, for the right item, for the right duration. Do that systematically, and it's the most efficient investment in your Vinted operation. Do it at random, and it's just money gone.
