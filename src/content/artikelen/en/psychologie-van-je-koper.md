A buyer sends a message. "Is the blue T-shirt still available, and if so, for how much?" Four minutes later the item is sold. The buyer hasn't negotiated, hasn't asked about condition, hasn't hesitated. She got confirmation that the price had just dropped, and that was enough.

Two hours earlier, another buyer had sent five messages. Questions about the measurements, about where the piece came from, about the state of the labels. She'd made an offer I thought was too low. I'd countered. She never replied again.

Those two people aren't random cases. They represent two fundamentally different ways of buying, and anyone who doesn't see that wastes time on the second type and loses chances with the first. Across more than 2,900 transactions, I've seen four buyer profiles recur. Not as theory but as a pattern in behaviour: how fast someone decides, what they write before buying, how they react when you counter, how satisfied they are afterwards.

Those four profiles together drive your revenue, your time spent, and your reputation. Each demands a different response, and treating them all the same costs you in missed conversions.

## The Essentialist

Forty percent of transactions. The quiet engine behind your cash flow.

They know what they're looking for before they even open your listing. A shirt in size M. A polo in white. A basic cable knit. They check the photos, verify the measurements, and buy, or they don't buy because the information was missing. Their decision lands within one to two hours of first contact. The average number of messages for a successful transaction with this profile is one to three. They're also the group most likely to buy without sending a single message.

What drives the Essentialist is efficiency. They don't want a surprise, a story, an enthusiastic description of how great the piece is. They want to know it fits, that it's in good condition, and that it arrives fast. If those three things are in the listing, the sale is basically made before they even message you.

This profile's conversion rate on a well-communicated listing runs above 70% at first contact. That's the highest of the four profiles, and also the easiest to influence. All you need to do is not get in the way. No unnecessary delay in your response, no long messages they have to wade through three lines to get through, no negotiating when the price is already reasonable. T-shirts and shirts, this profile's preferred items, run a 73% gross margin. They're not your most glamorous sales, but they're the reason you have positive cash flow at the end of the month.

What I've learned about the Essentialist: a direct price cut in the first message works better than a lengthy explanation. Don't explain why the item is worth the price, confirm that there's room and that it ships tomorrow. Those two facts are enough for this profile to decide.

## The Collector

Fifteen to twenty percent of buyers, but responsible for around thirty percent of revenue on premium pieces.

They search specifically. A Polo Bear sweater in good condition. An RL92 flag sweater with both labels intact. A vintage piece from a particular collection. They didn't stumble onto it. They've been searching, sometimes for weeks. One buyer once messaged me to ask if I'd remember her if I ever listed a Polo Bear sweater. She had a budget of €100 to €150 in mind. The sweater I eventually listed, I priced at €165. She bought it.

That's the psychology of the Collector in one transaction. They're price-insensitive on scarcity, but they hold high standards for authenticity. The questions they ask are targeted and technical. What season is this piece from? Is this intarsia or jacquard? They're not testing whether you're trustworthy, they're testing whether you know what you're talking about. Understand that, and you know how to run the conversation.

Information beats any price cut here. A Collector offering a lower price while still wanting proof of authenticity isn't reacting to a discount, she's reacting to evidence. Stating the piece's original retail price sets the baseline for the resale price. Adding four authenticity photos, label, wash label, logo, stitching, drops resistance to nearly zero. This profile's conversion rate, once authenticity is fully proven, sits around 95%. They accept the asking price once they're certain.

What I never do with a Collector: offer a discount when they hesitate. That signals that I doubt the piece's value myself, and a Collector feels that instantly. The right response to hesitation is more information, not a lower price.

## The Browser

Twenty-five to thirty percent of volume. Twenty percent of revenue. The most time-consuming group.

They generate more messages than anyone but convert the least per conversation. They send offers at 60 to 75% of the asking price. They like five items and buy none. They ask four questions and disappear. A common opener is simply: "Best price?" Another classic start is a bid so far below the asking price it feels like an insult, but that's not intent. It's a tactic.

The Browser isn't drawn to the item itself but to the illusion of a deal. They want to feel like they got something for less than it's worth. That's their primary motivation, and once you understand that, you stop trying to convince them of the item's quality. They're not interested in quality at the moment of negotiating. They want the satisfaction of the negotiation itself.

The most effective response to a low offer is neither defending nor giving in immediately. It's the split-the-difference tactic. State your price, their offer, and propose the middle. "I see €20. My price is €30. Let's split it at €25." That gives the Browser the feeling of having won a deal while the final price is still acceptable. The average gap between a Browser's first offer and the eventual sale price in a successful transaction sits around 20% of the original asking price. They get something, but not everything.

Drop to the asking price at the first lowball offer and you make a double mistake. First you lose margin. Second you train your buyer base: the next Browser sees that your items get cheaper with pressure and starts bidding lower right away. Across hundreds of transactions a year, that costs more than you'd think.

Another approach that works with the Browser is the bundle. Someone who liked five items responds well to a proactive message offering three items together at a twenty percent discount. They get their sense of a good deal, you raise average order value. That's a trade that works for both sides.

What the data also shows: accepting a first offer directly is less effective than countering, even when the margin is already good. A counteroffer signals value. Accepting immediately suggests the price was actually too high to begin with. The numbers are clear: the counteroffer strategy leads to an actual sale 1.6 times more often than accepting an opening bid immediately.

## The Minimalist

Ten to fifteen percent of buyers. The smallest group, but also the most consistent source of good reviews.

They buy deliberately. They ask questions that surprise beginners because they're not about price. "Is this 100% merino wool?" "Are there really no stains that show up less on the photos?" "How should I wash this?" They read the full description before asking a question, and the question they ask is always about something that wasn't in it.

The Minimalist doesn't buy a T-shirt, she buys a piece she wants to wear for three years. Merino versus acrylic isn't a detail to her, it's a purchase criterion. Mentioning that a shirt has been washed dozens of times and still looks good is relevant information. Sustainability arguments also work here, literally: buying second-hand as a deliberate choice, not a compromise.

Honesty about small flaws lowers the return risk with this profile and raises the final score. A Minimalist who later discovers a detail she'd have wanted to know doesn't write a five-star review. A Minimalist who knew everything and chose deliberately writes a review that consistently includes the phrases "neatly packaged" or "smells fresh." Those phrasings show up consistently in this profile's positive reviews.

## How to recognise who you're dealing with fast

The four profiles aren't hard to tell apart once you know what to look for.

An Essentialist doesn't ask, or asks one targeted question about measurements or shipping and nothing more. A Collector opens with a technical question about the item itself, something only someone with brand knowledge would notice. A Browser opens with an offer or a question that's really a negotiation. A Minimalist asks a question about material or care that wasn't in the description.

In practice, that means you pick an approach right after the first message. For the Essentialist: short and direct, logistical certainty first. For the Collector: expert tone, authenticity proof immediately available. For the Browser: calm, counteroffer or bundle, never emotional. For the Minimalist: honest, technical, no overselling.

## Response time isn't a courtesy, it's revenue

> Replying within an hour raises purchase likelihood by 40% compared to replying after 24 hours. That pattern holds consistently in the data across more than 2,500 transactions.

The reason is simple. An Essentialist who gets no answer moves on. Vinted always shows another listing, a competitor who might reply faster. On a platform with thousands of sellers offering the same item, wait time is a direct conversion killer. With the Collector, something else is at play: a slow response signals an unprofessional seller, and a Collector prefers to buy from someone who comes across as reliable. They have patience, but not endless patience.

Response times under fifteen minutes deliver a 55 to 65% conversion chance with the Essentialist. That's the achievable ceiling for this profile. Anyone who consistently replies fast during peak traffic hours maximises that chance without extra effort elsewhere.

## Geography changes buying behaviour. Why a buyer in Paris buys differently than one in Ghent

> The 2025 sales data shows a sharp geographic split: 55% of transactions come from Belgium, 20% from the Netherlands and 20% from France. Even though the items are identical, buying behaviour and review etiquette differ fundamentally by country.

Geography isn't a cultural nuance, it's a risk factor for your rating. A Belgian buyer buys on intuition and trust, a French buyer buys on evidence and technical precision. Anyone approaching every market with the same text leaves conversion on the table or risks unnecessary negative reviews.

Belgian and Dutch buyers are simpler to serve. They respond positively to direct communication, trust quickly, and leave high ratings when everything checks out. Dutch buyers are somewhat more price-sensitive but also faster in their decisions. My transaction history shows Dutch and Belgian buyers consistently score 4.88 or higher when shipping happens within 24 hours. For them, speed is the main measure of quality.

### The French standard

French buyers account for roughly 10% of my volume but require 25% more time in chat. The French market applies a stricter definition of service and product information. A French buyer doesn't see detailed information as a luxury, but as a baseline requirement to avoid a negative review. They demand absolute perfection in condition and photography.

This behaviour isn't reluctance, it's risk management from the buyer's side. French buyers are less impulsive and use chat to remove uncertainty. Detailed communication in your listings isn't a nice-to-have here, it's a necessity for conversion. Adapt your listing strategy for the French market by making the "authenticity stack" standard: photos of the QR code, the wash label with RN numbers, and the neck label. Give measurements proactively in the text, since that's the number one question French buyers ask before hitting "buy". Leaving out information hoping the buyer won't ask doesn't work: the French buyer always asks, and penalises a lack of proactivity in the star rating.

Systematically applying the "information hierarchy" (exact centimetres and detail photos) has led, in my case, to 30% fewer size-related questions. Transactions with a personal, transparent message also generate 27% higher review scores, essential for offsetting the demanding French threshold.

Across 2,537 sales this pattern has held consistently over multiple years, which shows geography is a hard factor in your risk profile.

## What reviews tell you about your buyer mix

Across 1,296 analysed reviews there's a pattern worth noting. The highest ratings consistently come from Collectors, who average 4.98, and from repeat buyers at 4.95. The lowest scores come from new accounts and Dutch-language buyers who asked a lot of questions before purchase. That second cluster, the Browser-like buyer, is responsible for 9.73% of the 1- and 2-star scores.

There's also a hard correlation of -0.64 between review length and score. The longer the text, the lower the rating, with one exception: the Collector.

Beginners invest their energy in the item itself. The photo, the price, the description. That's right. But they treat the communication afterwards as secondary, and respond to every profile the same way.

A Collector asks if the label is intact. The beginner answers in general terms about the piece's condition. The Collector wants proof, not reassurance. The conversation dies.

A Browser sends an offer at 60% of the asking price. The beginner gives a discount out of fear of losing the sale. The Browser senses that this works and bids even lower next time. Over a year, that pattern costs more than a beginner realises.

An Essentialist asks if it can ship tomorrow. The beginner sends a long answer about the item's condition, its origin, the reasons it's worth the price. The Essentialist walks away. Not because the information was wrong, but because she never asked for it.

The most common mistake isn't that beginners say the wrong thing. It's that they don't listen to what the buyer's first message already reveals about what that person needs. Every buyer profile announces itself in the opening of the conversation. Anyone who recognises that signal and plays to it converts consistently better than anyone who ignores it.
