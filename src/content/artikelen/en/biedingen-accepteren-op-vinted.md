Getting an offer feels like relief. Especially if it's not extremely low, the reflex is often: okay, accept and move on. I get that reflex. But it costs you money.

When I started analysing my own sales data, after more than 2,500 transactions on Vinted, one pattern stood out immediately. Listings where I accepted an offer instantly rarely got any more interaction afterwards. No new offers, few extra favourites, and no upward price dynamic. The negotiation was over, but not at the best price.

That's not a coincidence. It ties into something fundamental about how people judge value.

## What my data shows

The pattern was consistent. On listings where I didn't accept immediately but responded with a counteroffer, a short delay, or simply a message, I saw multiple offers more often. Those items also stayed visible in the feed longer, and the final sale price was, on average, higher.

The most striking part: buyers who opened with a low first offer often turned out willing to raise it once there was room left to negotiate. They stayed in the game, as long as I kept the door slightly open. The moment I slammed that door shut by accepting instantly, the dynamic disappeared.

The conclusion from my sales history isn't that accepting is always wrong. The conclusion is that responding structurally outperforms ending the conversation.

## Why accepting instantly works against you

People don't judge value objectively. They use the first number they see as the reference point for what's reasonable. This is called anchoring, and it's well documented in negotiation psychology.

When you accept an offer immediately, that offer becomes the norm. Not just for that buyer, but for how your item "feels" overall. Someone who views your listing afterwards and sees that you agree quickly draws the unconscious conclusion that there's not much tension around it. That the item isn't scarce. That there's room for a lower offer.

There's also a direct psychological effect on the buyer who just made the offer. If it's accepted instantly, part of them thinks: I could have gone lower. That thought undermines confidence in the transaction, not in the seller, but in themselves. And buyers who doubt their own decision leave lower reviews and ask more questions afterwards.

Accepting immediately also removes urgency. Urgency is exactly what drives people to act. An item that sits there casually waits for a buyer who's never in a hurry. Anyone who understands how a buyer actually decides, see [the psychology of your buyer](/en/articles/psychologie-van-je-koper/), recognises this mechanism instantly: it's rarely about the item itself, it's about the signal your behaviour sends.

## You're training your market without realising it

This is the part most beginners miss. Buyers adjust their behaviour based on what they've learned from you.

If you consistently accept quickly, buyers learn that opening low pays off. They put in less effort, because they don't need to. They know you'll say yes anyway. Over time you see it in your data: offers get systematically lower, while nothing about your listing itself has changed.

The reverse is also true. Sellers who consistently respond, counter, or wait a bit build a reputation as someone who doesn't agree without thinking. Buyers open higher when they know you won't bite immediately.

That's not an intimidation tactic. It's simply what happens when you apply a consistent approach.

## When it's fine to accept immediately

Almost never isn't never. There are situations where accepting right away is the smart move.

If the offer hits your target price exactly, there's little to gain by waiting. If the buyer is reliable, has bought from you before and pays instantly, certainty outweighs the chance of a few extra euros. If you need cash at that moment for a buying opportunity, quick liquidity beats maximum margin.

There are also categories I treat differently. I hold firm on cable knit sweaters and Polo Bear pieces. Those are scarce items with high demand, and buyers in that segment know it. They bid higher if you don't jump immediately. For a standard €18 T-shirt, the maths are different: the time you spend negotiating doesn't outweigh a possible €2 extra.

The rule of thumb I use: the scarcer the item, the slower I am to accept. The more it's a commodity category, the less negotiation is worth.

## What works better than accepting or declining

Most sellers think in two options: accept or decline. That's too limited.

A light counteroffer, 5 to 10 percent above the bid, sets a new anchor without pushing the buyer away. It shows you're negotiating, not that you're inflexible. I use a fixed structure for this: I briefly state why I'm holding my price, I make a concrete proposal, and I make it easy for the buyer to say yes. Something like: I can do €X, and I'll ship it today. That creates clarity on both sides.

Asking a question also works. Not as a trick, but as a filtering technique. The buyer who answers is serious. The buyer who drops off at a simple question wouldn't have bought anyway. Asking whether they've already tried the size or want more photos slows the process down in a way that costs you nothing and attracts serious buyers.

A deliberate pause, without a counteroffer or a decline, is sometimes enough. That one hour without a reply does something to a buyer's psychology. They start to wonder if the item will still be available. That doubt works in your favour.

And if you genuinely want to decline, do it in a way that doesn't close the door completely. Something like: I'll hold my asking price for now, but feel free to let me know if you'd like to propose something later. That keeps the buyer engaged without you giving anything away.

## Buyers respond differently depending on their type

Not every buyer deserves the same approach. That's something you learn after a few hundred transactions.

A buyer who's looking for a Polo Bear sweater and immediately asks about the neck label and the RN code is a collector. They buy as soon as authenticity is confirmed. Negotiating with that buyer costs you more than it earns, because they'll walk away the moment they feel you're making it difficult. Speed and substantive precision are the right answer there.

A buyer who asks whether the sweater fits like a small when the label says medium, then follows up with three shipping questions, is a different type. They're still orienting. That's the buyer where a small counteroffer has better odds than a quick acceptance, because they're looking for confirmation that they're getting a good deal.

Learning who's on the other end, based on what they ask and how fast they ask it, largely determines how you should respond. It doesn't always work out, but you learn to read it.

## In one sentence

Accepting closes a transaction. Responding keeps a conversation open. And in that conversation, higher offers, faster decisions and better final prices emerge. That's not a negotiation tactic from a business book. It's what my data consistently shows, transaction after transaction.
