> A description isn't a summary of an item. It's an answer to the questions a buyer is going to ask before or after the purchase, and the goal is to answer them before they're asked. Real value rarely sits in the physical object itself, but in the creation of context. A description acts as a filter that removes uncertainty before it becomes a barrier to the sale. Anyone who anticipates questions about size, condition and authenticity builds an invisible wall against returns and time-consuming chats.

## What causes 30% of all problems

Across my full transaction history, complaints and questions consistently cluster around five factors. Thirty percent are about sizing and fit. Twenty-five percent about the item's condition. Twenty percent about shipping time. Fifteen percent about authenticity on premium items. Ten percent about material or washing instructions.

Together, those five factors cover nearly every question a buyer can ask. They're predictable. And they're almost all preventable by proactively answering them in the description.

Cover all five factors consistently in every listing and you cut total question volume in chat by 67%. That's not a small improvement. Every question that never gets asked is a buyer who decides faster, hesitates less, and is less likely to drop off halfway. Fewer questions isn't laziness. It's conversion optimisation.

## Sizes are the only universal language

A clothing size is a label. Not a promise. A size M at Ralph Lauren fits differently than a size M at another brand. Slim fit and custom fit fit differently under the same label. Items washed years ago may have shrunk.

Centimetres are the only objective information. I measure every item flat and note four measurements. Pit-to-pit gives the width. Total length gives the drape. Sleeve length gives the fit in the arm. Shoulder width confirms whether the item hangs straight. Those four numbers put the responsibility for an accurate assessment with the buyer.

Systematically adding exact measurements has led to 38% fewer sizing questions in my chats. That figure has held consistently across years of data. The investment per listing is two minutes of measuring. The return is less time spent communicating, fewer incomplete transactions, and a lower chance of negative reviews.

## Condition demands aggressive honesty

> An item with a small stain on the inside of the collar that isn't mentioned in the description is a return risk. The same item with that mention added, plus a close-up photo, is a sale to a buyer who knows exactly what they're getting.

The urge to downplay or hide flaws is understandable but backfires. Buyers who discover something afterwards that wasn't disclosed are unhappy with the seller, not the item. Buyers who know about a flaw before the purchase and buy anyway are informed and satisfied once everything matches the description. Being transparent about flaws increases the chance of a five-star review on items with wear by 25%.

Tone matters here too. "Small mark on the inside of the collar, see photo 5, barely noticeable" is honest without undermining the item. "Has some signs of wear" without specifics is vague and invites questions. Specific and soft works better than general and neutral.

## Authenticity as your first line of defence

On items above €50, authenticity is the biggest source of buyer hesitation. Fifteen percent of all interactions on Polo Bear sweaters and flag sweaters are about authenticity. Collectors ask targeted questions: whether the neck label has the right font, whether the stitching quality checks out, whether the wash label shows the correct RN code. Beginners ask vaguer questions: "is this real?" without knowing what to look for.

My approach on every item above €50 is a fixed set of photos I call the authenticity stack: neck label, wash label with RN number, logo detail, and the yellow QR tag in the collar. Those four photos together answer 95% of authenticity concerns before they're even raised. They show the technical markers that reassure a buyer without me having to argue the case myself.

One practical warning: external links to verification pages can't go in the listing itself. Vinted can remove listings that contain external URLs. I only send additional proof via private chat if a buyer specifically asks for it.

## Material and care instructions aren't an afterthought

Ten percent of questions are about material and care. That sounds small, but it concentrates on higher-priced items with a specific buyer profile. Someone buying a merino cable knit wants to know if it's really merino. Someone buying a €60 cashmere piece wants to be able to care for it properly.

The word "merino" in a description increases conversion among quality-focused buyers by 15%. Not because it's a magic word, but because it's specific. "100% wool" says less than "100% merino wool, washed on a 30-degree wool cycle, dried flat." That information reduces the odds of a ruined sweater, which reduces the odds of a complaint, which increases the odds of a five-star review.

## The photo logic

Photos on Vinted aren't a portfolio. They're prevention.

The function of a photo isn't to make the item look pretty. It's to answer every visual question the buyer might have. Four questions dominate: what does the item look like as a whole? What do the label details show? Are there signs of wear, and if so, where? Does the colour match what I'm expecting?

The first photo shows the item laid flat on a neutral surface, in bright light, with no unnecessary props. The second shows the neck label up close. The third shows the wash label. The fourth and fifth show any wear in close-up. If there's no wear, the fourth shows a material detail and the fifth a different angle.

That sequence isn't an aesthetic choice. It's a protocol that prevents returns. A buyer who's seen all the labels, assessed the condition, and confirmed the colour is buying deliberately. The odds of a mismatch between expectation and reality are small.

I always photograph wear, even when it's minimal. A buyer who discovers a small bit of pilling that wasn't in the photos has a legitimate reason to be unhappy. A buyer who saw that same pilling in a close-up photo and bought anyway has no argument. The difference is in the transparency, not the item.

## The title as a search engine

A title on Vinted is also the search query the buyer types in. Someone searching for a "Ralph Lauren cable knit sweater navy M" only finds my listing if those words are in it. Someone who also searches in French, English or Italian only finds my listing if those languages are in it too.

My titles are multilingual because my buyer base is multilingual. A cable knit listing includes the Dutch and English terms, and the French one for the 20% French buyers. A title like "Polo Ralph Lauren kabel trui cable maglione jersey knit, navy donkerblauw bleu, maat M" covers every common search query in one line. It looks cluttered, but it's functional.

The word "winter sweater" in knitwear titles increases seasonal discoverability by 25%. That figure reflects that buyers start actively searching for warm clothing in August and September while supply hasn't fully caught up with that search behaviour yet. Use the word while competitors are still only writing "cable knit" and you're more discoverable during that transition period.

A strong title and description determine whether a listing gets found organically. ## Price as a consequence of the description

There's a direct link between the quality of a listing and the price it can achieve. A description with exact measurements, condition information, authenticity proof and material details justifies a higher asking price than a four-word description. Not because the buyer is paying for information, but because information removes doubt, and doubt is the biggest obstacle to accepting a higher price.

For cable knits, my median sale price is €47.50. Items with a full description, multilingual title and authenticity stack consistently sell at or above that median. Items with a thin description sell lower or slower. The five-minute investment per listing goes straight into the sale price.

## The trap

The most common mistake beginners make in listings is the opposite of writing too little. It's writing what they find nice about the item instead of what the buyer needs to decide.

"Beautiful vintage Ralph Lauren sweater in perfect condition, gorgeous colour and timeless design" contains zero usable information. No measurements, no condition details, no material. The buyer knows nothing more after that sentence than before. The chance of a sale is identical to a blank description.

The second mistake is the phrase "fits oversized" without centimetres. Oversized is subjective. For a buyer in Belgium, oversized means something different than for a buyer in Paris. Centimetres aren't subjective.

The third mistake is hiding wear. Not out of bad intent, but out of the misconception that being transparent about flaws scares buyers off. The opposite is true. An item that arrives better than described earns five stars. An item that arrives worse than described earns one star and sometimes a refund. My estimated return rate sits at 3 to 5% across my total transaction volume. That figure is directly tied to the quality of the listing that preceded the purchase.

You write a description once. You carry the consequences until the item is sold.
