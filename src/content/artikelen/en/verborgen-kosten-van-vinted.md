*Analysis based on 770 of my own sales (2024-2025), part of a dataset of more than 2,500 transactions since 2020.*

If you sell on Vinted, you'll hear often that the platform charges "0% commission." That's literally true. Vinted charges you nothing. What does happen: the buyer pays a buyer protection fee of a fixed €0.70 plus 5% of the item price, separate from shipping. That fee shows up nowhere on your payout. It shows up on the buyer's screen, at the exact moment they're about to click "buy."

That makes it a hidden cost in the literal sense: hidden from you as the seller, visible to the buyer at exactly the moment that matters. And that cost doesn't scale linearly. The cheaper your item, the more it weighs relatively.

## The maths nobody shows you

The formula is fixed: €0.70 + 5% of the item price. Put that next to a few real price points and the pattern jumps out.

| Item price | Buyer protection fee | Fee as % of price |
| --- | --- | --- |
| €5 | €0.95 | 19.0% |
| €10 | €1.20 | 12.0% |
| €20 | €1.70 | 8.5% |
| €30 | €2.20 | 7.3% |
| €50 | €3.20 | 6.4% |
| €100 | €5.70 | 5.7% |

Just the fixed part (€0.70) alone: on a €5 item, that's 14% of the price. On a €50 item, barely 1.4%. The same €0.70, a completely different weight.

That's not a footnote. That's why an €8 T-shirt suddenly feels like €9.10 at checkout, while a €50 sweater barely jumps to €53.20. The buyer doesn't experience a fixed amount, they experience a percentage on top of what they'd already budgeted for. And on cheap items, that percentage is highest, exactly where the buyer has the least patience to accept it.

## So what's actually left of your €100

Worth straightening out first: that buyer protection fee doesn't come out of your €100. You ask €100, you receive €100. What comes off that €100 sits on your side of the ledger, not Vinted's: your buying cost, what you paid for the piece yourself. That's it. No platform commission, no listing fee.

So the honest question isn't "what does Vinted cost me on €100 in revenue," because the answer is: nothing, directly. The honest question is: what does that invisible cost do to the buyer's odds of ever actually getting you that €100. And that's where it gets interesting, because my own numbers follow exactly the same pattern as the fee table above, just via a completely different route.

## My own margins follow the same curve

In my 2024-2025 sales data (770 items), I looked per price segment at what I kept net, separate from the buyer protection fee. Purely my own sale price minus my own buying cost.

| Segment | Number of sales | Avg. sale price | Avg. net margin |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| < €10 | 21 (2.7%) | €7.48 | 25.0% |
| €10-20 | 54 (7.0%) | €16.03 | 51.7% |
| €20-30 | 162 (21.0%) | €25.70 | 60.3% |
| €30-40 | 186 (24.2%) | €34.09 | 62.2% |
| €40-50 | 201 (26.1%) | €44.25 | 62.3% |
| €50-60 | 75 (9.7%) | €53.38 | 62.9% |
| €60+ | 71 (9.2%) | €85.47 | 66.8% |

This is the core of the article. Below €10, I keep an average of 25% margin. From €20 on, it stabilises around 60%. The reason is simple: my buying cost per piece doesn't scale down with the sale price. A basic T-shirt and a rare sweater often cost me roughly the same at purchase, but sell for very different amounts. On the cheap piece, that fixed buying cost eats up a much bigger share of the proceeds.

And that's exactly the same mechanism as the buyer protection fee, just on my side of the table. On cheap items I'm doubly disadvantaged: my own margin is thinnest in that segment, and the buyer pays proportionally the most in fees on top of the price. Two independent effects that point to the exact same conclusion: selling standalone below a certain price threshold is a bad deal for everyone at the table.

It's also no coincidence that only 9.7% of my sales sit under €20. That segment sells worse, and when it does sell, it earns little. The market simply confirms what the maths already says.

## Why this affects your price and speed, even though you don't pay the fee

The buyer protection fee doesn't touch your bank account directly. But it does touch the buyer's decision, and that decision determines how fast your stock turns over.

Two mechanisms play out here. **Anchoring at checkout.** The buyer forms their expectation based on the listed item price, the same anchoring effect that also plays into [bidding on Vinted](/en/articles/biedingen-accepteren-op-vinted/). At checkout they suddenly see a higher total. On a €50 item, that jump is small enough to ignore. On a €5 item, the jump to €5.95 is relatively large, large enough to trigger hesitation, drop-off, or an attempt to negotiate a lower price to offset the feeling of "loss." You don't feel that as a cost, you feel it as a slower-selling item or a buyer asking for a discount.

**Diluting the deal.** A buyer hesitating over a cheap standalone item stops hesitating the moment they can combine it with something else. Not because the total price suddenly stops mattering, but because the fixed fee then spreads across more value. Which brings us to the practical rule.

## The point

Vinted charges you nothing. That's not a marketing line, it's literally true. But "paying nothing" and "bearing no cost" are two different things. The cost shifts to the buyer, and the buyer translates that cost into their decision to buy or not, especially on cheap items. My own data confirms that from a different angle: in exactly the segment where the buyer pays proportionally the most in fees, I also keep the least myself. Selling standalone under €20 is the worst version of the deal for both parties.
