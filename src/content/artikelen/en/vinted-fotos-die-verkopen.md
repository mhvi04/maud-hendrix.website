Photography in reselling isn't a creative process. It's risk reduction. A buyer on Vinted can't feel an item, can't try it on, can't inspect it up close. Everything they know comes from your photos. Every doubt a photo fails to remove becomes either a question in your messages, or a reason to keep scrolling. This isn't an aesthetic choice, it's a series of decisions that measurably affect your conversion, and my own complaint data shows it directly: condition and material together explain 35 percent of every dispute I've ever had, and both are problems good photography can largely prevent.

## Two seconds to get the click

More than 90 percent of Vinted buyers use the app on mobile. In the feed, someone sees a thumbnail, and within under two seconds decides whether to click through or keep scrolling. That's the narrowest point in your entire sales funnel. A strong description, exact measurements or a fair price have zero effect if the first photo doesn't earn that click.

What works in that first photo: the item fully visible, laid flat on a neutral, light background, sharp and evenly lit, ideally in natural daylight. The colour has to match reality, and the logo has to be recognisable. What works against you: a cluttered background, a wrinkled item, a dark corner of a bedroom. A neutral background isn't a style choice, it keeps attention on the garment and stops it drifting anywhere else. The difference between a clean hero photo and a quick shot in a dark room isn't just about looks. A sharp first photo gives someone enough to click through on, and without that click, your measurements, your description and every other photo you have don't matter at all.

## The fixed five-photo structure

Every listing I put up follows the same order, and that order isn't arbitrary, it's the result of what consistently converts better.

The first photo is the hero shot, the frontal view in daylight described above. After the main photo, I show the item from multiple angles, and the back view is a standard part of that. On a shirt, jacket or sweater, the back gives important information about fit, length, fabric and any wear not visible from the front. A buyer who only sees the front is left with an incomplete picture, and that's exactly the kind of uncertainty that leads to questions or disputes later.

Next come the detail photos of fabric and finish. On a cable knit, I photograph the knit pattern up close. On a shirt, I show the weave, the collar, the cuffs and the buttons. On a T-shirt or polo, I focus on the neckband, the hem and the logo embroidery. These photos give the buyer a sense of texture, density and quality without being able to touch the item.

Step four is the authenticity stack: a set of photos that together support the item's assessment. At minimum I show the neck label, the wash label and the logo. On newer items I add a photo of a digital tag or QR code when present. On pricier items, or pieces that get counterfeited more often, I take more detail photos than on a standard T-shirt: the motif up close, the placement of the embroidery, the seam quality and relevant label details. Collectors often zero in on exactly those details, and if you're asking a higher price, you need to show what it's based on. On items above €50, this stack is never optional for me.

The fifth and final photo is the transparency photo: every flaw, sharp and well lit, never from an angle where the buyer has to hunt for it. A slight discolouration on a cuff, minor pilling on the sleeve, or a barely visible stain near the hem needs to be in a photo and explicitly named in the description too.

## The logo deserves its own photo

On branded clothing, and especially Ralph Lauren, the logo isn't just a recognition mark. It's a feature buyers use to judge authenticity and condition. A sharp detail photo of the logo shows exactly how the embroidery looks: loose threads, wear, a faded motif. You don't hide that kind of detail by leaving it out, you show it deliberately, because otherwise it only surfaces after the purchase anyway, as a complaint instead of a photo.

## Show measurements, don't just state them

A tape-measure photo isn't a replacement for the numbers in your description, it's a visual confirmation of them. The text still needs to clearly state the exact centimetres: pit-to-pit, total length, sleeve length, and for shirts and jackets, the shoulder width. But a photo showing the tape measure actually laid against the garment proves you really measured it and that the information isn't pulled from a generic size chart. For shirts, jackets and other pieces where fit depends heavily on shoulders or sleeves, multiple tape-measure photos can be useful. You don't need to photograph every centimetre of a garment, just the measurements that make the difference for that specific model.

The effect isn't marginal. Size-related questions and complaints make up 30 percent of all interactions in my chat history, by far the single biggest source of friction. Exact centimetre measurements instead of a vague claim like "fits like an M" lead to noticeably fewer pre-purchase questions. Fewer questions means less time per item sold and a faster turnaround, which directly affects your operational efficiency.

## What to check before you shoot

Not every flaw is a dealbreaker, but every undisclosed flaw is a potential complaint. Some flaws the market tolerates and some it doesn't, and the difference is rarely the severity of the flaw itself, it's whether you show it. Underarm marks on shirts almost always trigger a complaint if you don't photograph and mention them, because shirt buyers are noticeably critical of exactly that zone. Collar discolouration is something buyers notice immediately. Micro-stains on light colours are invisible in a mediocre photo but stand out all the more in daylight on arrival, so always photograph light pieces in the brightest light you have. Pilling on heavy knitwear sells fine as long as you show it, becomes a problem the moment you hide it. And a smell, cigarette smoke, perfume, or long-term storage, is something a photo can't show but that you do need to fix first: wash and air an item twice before photographing it if there's even a hint of odour. A buyer writing after delivery that the package smells is a complaint that was entirely avoidable before you even hit the button.

## Transparency is a conversion tool, not a concession

The reflex with a small flaw is often to hide it or photograph it unclearly. That's a mistake. Buyers who see a small flaw explicitly named and photographed actually trust the rest of the description more than buyers who only read the word "perfect." In my experience, transparent documentation of flaws, photo included, correlates with roughly a 70 percent drop in post-sale complaints. Fewer complaints means fewer refunds and fewer negative reviews, which matters more in the long run than the small risk that a buyer walks away because of that flaw. My own average rating of 4.81 out of 5 is a direct result of that approach, not of perfect clothing, but of honest photos.

## What this ultimately means

The difference between a listing with three mediocre photos and one with five strong, well-structured photos isn't cosmetic. It's financial. Better photos lower the risk a buyer feels, raise the odds they click through, and cut the number of questions you have to answer afterwards. Fewer questions means less time per item, a faster sale means better cash flow, and that whole chain starts in the same two seconds someone decides whether to click through or not.
