A notification pops up: someone favourited your item. Most sellers do nothing at that point, they just wait to see if a message or an offer follows. That's not wrong, but it's not the most deliberate response either. A heart is more than a nice signal, it's usable information, and what you do with it in the hours after can be the difference between a sale and a missed opportunity.

## What a heart actually means

Not every heart carries the same weight, and it helps to make that distinction right away. On pricier, eye-catching pieces, a heart functions for some people as a kind of visual bookmark, similar to saving something to a mood board. They like it, but have no concrete purchase intent at that moment. On an entry-price item like a T-shirt it's different: there, a heart is usually a direct signal of real interest, simply because there's little reason to save a cheap, practical piece purely for inspiration.

That distinction determines how urgently you should respond.

| Item type | What a heart usually means | Recommended response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entry price (T-shirt, basic) | Direct signal of real interest | Stay quickly reachable, chance of a fast purchase |
| Premium / rare piece | Visual bookmark within a longer maturation process | Patience, don't force action |

## The window is shorter than you think

In my own data, there's an average of 1.8 days between the moment someone favourites an item and the moment of purchase. That's a short window. Most seriously interested buyers decide relatively fast, within that day-and-a-half to two-day span. If you simply wait without doing anything, that window often passes with nothing happening, and the odds that the same person spontaneously comes back a week later are small.

That's why passively waiting isn't the strongest strategy, especially not on items where you notice interest concentrating in a short period. The window where most decisions get made is exactly the window where you, as the seller, can still exert influence.

## When to take the initiative yourself

For a single heart on a common item, it's usually enough to just stay reachable and quick in case a question or offer follows. But once you notice an item collecting several hearts in a short time without any contact following, a proactive message is a strong move. I do this myself regularly for items liked multiple times within the same period: a short, friendly message asking if there's still interest, sometimes combined with a small offer like a bundle discount if the buyer has also liked other items of mine.

That last part works well because it turns the passive interest of a heart into an active negotiation, right within the window where the item still benefits fully from its freshness boost, the same momentum effect that also [drives how boosting works](/en/articles/boosten-op-vinted/). Someone who's liked multiple pieces from your listings without buying is often genuinely interested, but simply needs a nudge to make the decision.

## What not to do: giving in too fast

If a heart is followed by an offer, the reflex to accept it immediately is understandable but usually not the best move. Accepting instantly effectively lowers the item's perceived value in the buyer's eyes: it feels to them as if they might have bid even lower, or that the item wasn't as sought-after as they thought. A brief counteroffer, or simply a friendly confirmation that you're considering the offer and will get back to them, keeps the negotiation open and, in my experience, more often leads to a better final result than accepting right away. That's even more true when an item has multiple hearts or a lot of views: that indicates broader interest, and that interest gives you a bit more room not to fold for the first offer.

## A heart is also market information

Beyond the direct sales opportunity, a heart has another function: it tells you something about demand. If a particular type of item, a specific colour, or a certain size structurally collects more hearts than the rest of your listings, that's a signal to deliberately look for more of that type on your next buying round. I informally track which pieces attract hearts unusually fast and in high numbers, and use that as one of the inputs for what I buy in next. A heart isn't just a chance at an individual sale then, it's also free market research on what your audience currently finds attractive.

## What to do when nothing follows

If an item gets hearts but a purchase never materialises, even after a targeted attempt at contact, it's worth checking whether the pattern fits the category. For a T-shirt or shirt with a few hearts and no sale after a week, that's a reason to check the listing itself: are the exact measurements included, is the price in line with comparable pieces? For a premium or rare piece, a longer period of accumulating hearts without a sale is completely normal, and patience is more appropriate than intervening.

Also don't forget that a later price drop automatically sends a notification to everyone who ever favourited the item. That means every heart you collect today is a potential recipient of a reactivation message down the line, even if it doesn't lead to a direct sale now. A heart is rarely fully lost then, it stays an address in your audience you can reach out to again at a later, more strategic moment.

## In short

> A heart on a common, affordable item is usually a strong signal of real interest and deserves a quick response from you. A heart on a premium piece belongs to a longer, normal maturation process and calls for patience instead. Remember that most decisions happen within about 1.8 days of the heart, so waiting without action often lets that window pass unused. When an item gets multiple hearts, consider proactive contact, possibly with a bundle offer. Don't reflexively accept an offer right away, and use the pattern of which items attract hearts as input for your next buying round.
