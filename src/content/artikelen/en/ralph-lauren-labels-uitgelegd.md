One name, around twenty different lines, and a price gap that runs from five euros to more than a thousand for pieces that look like the same brand at first glance. Ralph Lauren isn't a brand in the ordinary sense. It's a layered system of sub-lines built up over almost sixty years, and anyone sourcing secondhand without knowing that system overpays or leaves money on the table. The label at the neck tells you which layer you're in. Learn to read that label and you'll know within three seconds whether you're looking at a ten-euro item or a two-hundred-euro one.

This article walks through every line you'll realistically run into on Vinted, in a thrift store, or from a bulk supplier. Not as a history lesson but as a tool. For each line: what it is, how to recognise it, and what it's worth on resale.

## How one bag designer built an empire with twenty labels

Ralph Lauren, born in 1939 as Ralph Lifshitz in the Bronx, didn't start with clothing in 1967 but with neckties. Wide, unconventional ties he sold under the name Polo after first working for Brooks Brothers. A full menswear collection followed in 1968, in 1971 he introduced the Polo player emblem on the cuff of a women's shirt, and in 1972 the short cotton polo shirt appeared in twenty-four colours. That shirt became the cornerstone of the entire American preppy look.

What Lauren sold was never just the garment. "I don't sell clothing, I sell a lifestyle." That statement explains why the brand could branch out so extremely. Every segment of the market, from couture to department store, got its own translation of the same dream. The result is a brand architecture that at one point consisted of seventeen different sub-labels. In recent years Ralph Lauren has deliberately merged some of them because even loyal customers no longer knew who was supposed to buy which label. For you as a reseller, that maze is exactly where the margin sits.

## The luxury top: where the biggest amounts sit

### Purple Label

Purple Label sits at the very top and is by far the most expensive men's line. Launched in 1994 as a premium menswear collection inspired by Savile Row tailoring in London. Pieces are often made in Italy by skilled tailors, in small runs, with materials you won't find anywhere else in the Ralph Lauren range. A Purple Label made-to-measure suit can cost more than five thousand dollars new, and even a pair of socks starts around a hundred dollars. Joe Biden wore a blue Purple Label suit on his first day in the White House, described by the brand itself as a symbolic statement.

The neck label is purple with gold text, and the branding is deliberately minimal. Purple Label often uses a distinct, more subtle pony motif instead of the classic embroidered horse. On resale this line retains relatively strong value, regularly more than sixty percent of retail price for suits and coats in good condition. See this label, and you pause before pricing out of habit.

### Ralph Lauren Collection

Ralph Lauren Collection is the women's equivalent of Purple Label and just as pricey. This is the line that walks the runway during fashion weeks, with the finest materials and the most handwork. Many resellers know Purple Label but don't recognise Collection, even though it sits in the same price bracket. A missed Collection dress or blazer is an expensive mistake.

### Black Label

Black Label arrived in 2005 as a modern luxury line positioned between Polo and Purple. Sleeker, contemporary silhouettes aimed at a younger, fashion-conscious audience unwilling to pay Purple Label prices. Tailored suits, leather jackets, smart casuals. The line was discontinued around 2014, and some models moved over to Purple Label afterward. The neck label is black with white or silver text. Resale value is mixed: since the line is gone, tailored pieces can fetch something, but brand recognition with the general public is limited, so you need a buyer who knows what Black Label was.

### Blue Label

Blue Label is confusing because the term means two things. In most buyers' mouths, "Blue Label" is simply the nickname for the regular blue neck label of Polo Ralph Lauren, the main line. Separately, there was an actual Blue Label line sold mainly in the Japanese market, a luxury women's line with a quality level approaching Purple Label, often carrying "Made in Japan" on the label. That Japanese version is rare outside Asia and interesting to collectors, but hard to sell if your buyers don't know the brand. Always check whether you're dealing with the Japanese luxury line and not just a standard Polo item someone is calling "blue label."

## Polo Ralph Lauren: the engine of your stock

Polo Ralph Lauren is what most people mean when they say the brand's name. The main line, active since 1967, and the workhorse of the whole system. The embroidered pony, the blue neck label with silver or gold text, the polos and sweaters and oxford shirts and varsity jackets. This is your volume. Polo has the broadest demand and the fastest turnover, and that's also where the catch is: margin per piece is average, sometimes low, but it sells.

A good polo shirt goes for thirty to fifty euros. An iconic sweater or a varsity jacket climbs to sixty to a hundred. Vintage cotton from the nineties performs better than modern pieces because the fabric was heavier, and buyers who know that seek it out specifically. Polo is also the most counterfeited Ralph Lauren label there is, so authentication is not an afterthought here. One thing beginners often get wrong: a blue label doesn't automatically mean vintage. That label was used well into the 2000s, and mislabelling a modern item as vintage gets you complaints from buyers who know the difference.

Within the Polo family sit a number of sub-lines and motifs you need to be able to place separately.

### Polo Bear

The Polo Bear is the teddy bear in various outfits, from tuxedo to Ivy League to ski suit. That bear isn't a separate line but a motif that appears on Polo pieces, and it significantly raises the value. An original vintage Polo Bear sweater from the nineties can go toward eight hundred euros with collectors, while a modern version of the same bear sits around a hundred to a hundred and fifty euros. Recognising the difference between those two amounts is literally your margin.

### Pink Pony

Pink Pony is the pink version of the pony logo, launched in 2000 as a global cancer awareness campaign. At least twenty-five percent of the sale price goes to cancer care, and on certain pieces like the cable-knit hoodie, sometimes a full hundred percent. The label carries its own shade of blue, distinct from the standard Polo label. Price-wise, Pink Pony sits at the lower end of the Polo range. Good to recognise, but not an item you charge a premium for.

### Polo Golf and RLX Golf

Polo Golf started around 1990 and focuses on smart golf wear: trousers, polos and fine knits. RLX Golf arrived in 1998 as the more technical variant with performance fabrics. Both usually carry the blue Polo label with a golf mention. The market is limited to golfers, so selective buying is the rule here, only on standout pieces or a very low purchase price.

### Polo Sport

Polo Sport is the wildcard of the nineties. Launched in 1992 with bold logos and loud prints, exclusively sportswear: windbreakers, sweatshirts, tees, caps and polos. The style became a streetwear icon with younger buyers. Today Polo Sport only appears as a capsule within the main line. Vintage pieces from the nineties have become collector's items, and a good windbreaker or anorak from that period fetches forty to eighty euros with the right buyer. Watch out, there's a lot of counterfeit Polo Sport in circulation. Don't confuse it with a separate "Ralph Lauren Sport" tag in navy with white letters, that's just an alternative label style for the same Polo Sport.

### RLX

RLX emerged around 1998 as a technical spin-off of Polo Sport, aimed at running, snowboarding, skiing and climbing, with waterproof fabrics and functional construction. RLX also supplied uniforms for the US Olympic team. It's performance wear, not luxury, with cult status among Polo collectors for the older technical pieces. Modern RLX performs modestly on resale.

### Polo Ralph Lauren Children

Finally there's simply Polo Ralph Lauren Children, with the same quality as the adult line. Children's sizes of iconic pieces like cashmere cable sweaters are sometimes bought by adults with smaller frames, so don't automatically underestimate them.

## RRL: the most honest brand in the whole universe

Double RL, written as RRL, launched in 1993 and carries the initials of Ralph and his wife Ricky. It's Lauren's personal passion project, inspired by his ranch in Colorado, by the workwear of gold prospectors and ranch hands from the late nineteenth century, and by military clothing from the American Civil War and the world wars. Selvedge denim, leather jackets, checked work shirts, fringed suede.

Quality ranges from solid to very high depending on the piece, and some items are made in the US. RRL reportedly runs at a loss for the company, but survives because it sits so close to Lauren's own taste. The neck label is ivory or brown with "Double RL" or "RRL". An RRL pair of jeans costs more than three hundred dollars new, and certain jackets run toward four thousand five hundred dollars. On resale, RRL retains forty to sixty percent of retail price, but the target audience is small and the sales cycle long. You need connoisseurs, and they exist, but they're not active on every platform. Buy RRL only in good condition and only when the niche aesthetic is right.

### Polo Country

Polo Country is the spiritual predecessor of RRL, born out of a short-lived Polo Country Store in the nineties. The same rugged Americana inspiration as RRL but older and rarer. An interesting label to recognise, since confusing it with ordinary Polo undersells its value.

### Polo Western Wear

Polo Western Wear was a brief collaboration where Ralph designed and Gap produced. The project stumbled over fit issues and Gap losing money on it, so the line disappeared from shelves quickly. Rare, mostly a curiosity.

## The bottom tier: almost always leave it

### Lauren Ralph Lauren (Green Label)

Lauren Ralph Lauren is the department-store line for women, launched as Lauren for Women in 1996, with sportswear, denim, dresses and accessories at a lower price point. There's also a Lauren menswear line with suits and shirts. Price-wise it sits above Chaps but below Polo. The line is also known as Green Label because the main label was dark green, though more modern pieces now use a black label. Ralph himself has no design input here. On Vinted these items fetch five to twenty euros, which makes them uninteresting unless you buy them in bulk and dirt cheap. A common mistake: the black Lauren label gets confused with the luxury Black Label, and buyers who mistake a cheaply produced item for top quality leave low reviews afterward.

### Chaps

Chaps was founded by Lauren himself in 1978 as a cheaper alternative against the rising counterfeit market, with bold stripes, heavy sweaters and collegiate spell-out clothing you now find en masse in vintage stores from the eighties and nineties. Chaps hasn't been directly connected to Ralph for years, and his name has disappeared from the label, it's now simply called Chaps and sold under licence. There's a trap here: many sellers list later, non-RL-affiliated Chaps pieces as "Chaps Ralph Lauren." Modern quality is low and resale value accordingly. Leave Chaps unless you find something genuinely old and special for a few euros.

### Polo Jeans Co., Denim & Supply, Ralph by Ralph Lauren and Polo University Club

Polo Jeans Co. was a licensed line that existed until 2006, made by an external manufacturer, recognisable by the American flag logo with the letters RL where the stars go. Construction was generally decent but fabric quality weak. Denim & Supply ran from 2011 until its discontinuation in 2016: boho, distressed denim and American flag motifs at an affordable price point, solid but unremarkable. Ralph by Ralph Lauren arrived around 1994 for the "hip and young" market and was mostly made in China, not junk but entry level. Polo University Club is a vintage entry-level line from the eighties, made under licence, mostly tailoring, where you sometimes find surprisingly nice fabrics for cheap but basic construction. For all of these: nothing to charge a premium for, generally leave them unless the purchase price is so low that even a small sale price still pays off.

A brand that belongs in the story but isn't a sub-line: **Club Monaco** was bought by Ralph Lauren Corporation in 1999 and sold again in 2021. It was never a diffusion line of Ralph Lauren, it stayed its own brand with its own identity, even during its years under RL. The best-made pieces, according to connoisseurs, date from before the acquisition, when much was produced in Canada. Good to know if you come across the label, but don't count it as part of the Ralph Lauren hierarchy.

## Reading the label: colour, text and the details that matter

The fastest authentication and value check starts with the colour of the neck label. Purple with gold is Purple Label, the top tier. Black with white or silver is Black Label, the discontinued luxury line. Blue with silver or gold text is the standard Polo line, your volume. Dark green is the old Lauren Ralph Lauren, while modern Lauren uses a black label you shouldn't confuse with Black Label. Ivory or brown with "Double RL" is RRL. That colour code puts you in the right price bracket nine times out of ten.

| Neck label colour | Line | Positioning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purple with gold | Purple Label | Luxury top, menswear |
| Black with white/silver | Black Label (discontinued) | Former modern luxury line |
| Blue with silver/gold | Polo Ralph Lauren | Main line, your volume |
| Dark green | Lauren Ralph Lauren (old) | Department-store line, low price |
| Black | Lauren Ralph Lauren (modern) | Department-store line, not Black Label |
| Ivory/brown, "Double RL" | RRL | Niche, high value with connoisseurs |

The pony logo itself is an unreliable guide, and that's where many beginners go wrong. There's a persistent story that the pony's colour signals exclusivity, but in practice designers mostly pick those colours on aesthetics. The pony colour doesn't tell you what the piece is worth. The label does.

The wash labels carry a lot of proof of authenticity. Authentic Ralph Lauren pieces often carry multiple wash labels with full care instructions in several languages, since the brand manufactures globally. A short, single-language wash label is suspicious. On those labels you'll regularly find an RN number, with RN 41381 being one of the company's known registration numbers, or a CA number. Vintage wash labels were often blue, while white ones have appeared since the 2010s too, always with sharp print quality. Sloppy or printed labels with blurry text are a red flag.

One period signal that matters more and more: modern Ralph Lauren pieces have sometimes carried a QR code on the inner label since around 2019. Find a QR code, and you're looking at recent production, not vintage. Conversely, an embroidered, almost illegibly stitched wash label can actually be a sign of authenticity and age, especially on the Rugby line where neck labels vary a lot.

Finally, the outlet question. A label with just "Ralph Lauren" and the pony, without "Polo", has long been associated with outlet and factory stock, similar to how Brooks Brothers had its 346 line. Someone from the regular Polo retail channel nuances that: this label also shows up on older core stock like oxfords and polos in standard colours, and is said to have been phased out by now. So it isn't always outlet, but it is a sign you're not dealing with the main line in its ordinary form. A small gold dot on a Polo label also points to an outlet piece.

## What this means for your sourcing

Look at the label first, always. That's not a routine step but the decisive move that determines your margin. The practical order from top to bottom is clear.

Purple Label, Ralph Lauren Collection and the main Polo Ralph Lauren line are your primary focus, the first two for margin and the third for volume. RRL and Polo Country are the high-value niche for anyone who knows the buyers. Vintage Polo Sport, Rugby, Polo Bear pieces and the rare Polo Country are the wildcards you search for specifically. Black Label and the Japanese Blue Label are only worth picking up on truly special pieces. Everything from Lauren Ralph Lauren, modern Chaps, Polo Jeans Co., Denim & Supply, Ralph by Ralph Lauren and Polo University Club you generally leave, unless the purchase price is so low that even a five-to-twenty-euro sale price still pays off.

> Ralph Lauren has built a brand structure over nearly six decades that's opaque to anyone who doesn't know it. The buyer who can tell the difference between a purple and a green label, between a vintage Polo Bear and a modern reproduction, between genuine Japanese Blue Label and an item someone just calls that, holds exactly the information edge that separates a ten-euro piece from a two-hundred-euro one.
